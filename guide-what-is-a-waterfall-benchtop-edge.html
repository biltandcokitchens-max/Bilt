What is a
waterfall edge?

The benchtop material does not stop at the edge — it turns the corner and runs to the floor.

The short answer

A waterfall edge continues the benchtop material vertically down the end of an island, cabinet run or peninsula to the floor, rather than stopping at a standard front edge profile — as though the stone or stone-look surface is "falling" over the edge. It reads as a single continuous slab even where two or three pieces have been joined with a mitred seam.

Why it needs a mitred join, done well

A convincing waterfall edge depends entirely on the mitre — the diagonal join where the horizontal benchtop meets the vertical drop — being cut and fitted so the veining or pattern lines up and the seam is as close to invisible as the material allows. A poorly cut mitre is the single most common way a waterfall edge looks cheap rather than expensive; see what a mitred join is.

Where it works, and where it is wasted

It suits an island or peninsula end that is genuinely visible from the main sightlines of the room — a kitchen you walk into and see the end grain of before anything else. On a run against a wall, where nobody will ever see the end, it is cost spent on a detail nobody experiences.

Ask us directly →
Does a waterfall edge cost much more?

Yes, noticeably — it uses significantly more of the benchtop material and requires precise mitre cutting and matching, which is skilled, time-consuming work.

Can any benchtop material do a waterfall edge?

Engineered stone, porcelain and natural stone all can; laminate generally cannot achieve a convincing seamless look at the mitre the same way.

Is a waterfall edge purely decorative?

Mostly, though it does protect the cabinet end panel underneath from knocks in a busy kitchen, which is a minor practical benefit alongside the visual one.
